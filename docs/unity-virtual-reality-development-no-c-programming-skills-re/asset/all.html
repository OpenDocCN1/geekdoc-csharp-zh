IN THIS SECTION , you'll set up everything for Virtual Reality and learn about VRTK, the Virtual Reality Toolkit, version 4, by Extend Reality Ltd. VRTK is available for free and has been licensed under the MIT license.
VIDEO LECTURE: ADVANTAGES of using VRTK 4 over alternate SDK's for VR Development
VRTK is not the only solution available, for getting started in virtual reality development. Unity, Oculus and Steam VR provide their own SDK’s for VR development.
It’s also possible to purchase assets from the asset store, to avail the VR specific functionality you need.
Unity’s XR Interaction Toolkit, is a high-level component based, interaction system, for creating VR experiences, and will eventually, integrate the various platforms. Oculus and Steam VR on the other hand, provide SDK’s for their own respective platforms.
The biggest advantage of VRTK, is that it provides a simple, yet extensible framework, that works across multiple platforms. It is also, both free, and open source.
VRTK is a Virtual Reality Development Toolkit as a whole, providing a wide range of support, already built in, for the core components in virtual reality, such as locomotion, interacting with 3D objects, as well as 2D and 3D controls.
This reduces the amount of code, you need to write, just to get started, in virtual reality Development, as VRTK does all the heavy lifting for you.
Now you can concentrate, on developing the parts of your game or application, without having to worry about, input management, character controllers, locomotion methods, climbing and grabbing mechanics, UI interactions, and a lot of other fundamentals, you would come to expect, from a VR framework.

CHAPTER SIX
Movement in VR
MOVEMENT IS AN essential part of any VR experience and the most common way to move a player is via teleportation. The main reason why teleportation is so popular in VR, is because it helps to avoid motion sickness. Motion sickness occurs when there’s a mismatch, between what you see and what you feel.
Teleportation is not the only way to move around in your Virtual world and VRTK provides you with quite a few alternatives, which you will look at in this section.
In this section you will look at the following modes of movement provided by VRTK, namely:
  • Instant Teleport
  • Dash Teleport
  • Teleport Targets to traverse large distances
  • Slide movement with smooth rotation
  • Slide movement with snap rotation
  • Dash or Warp movement with Snap rotation
  • Arm swinging movement
  • Climbing objects
  • Using a Pseudo body
VIDEO LECTURE: INSTANT and Dash Teleportation
Teleportation is a popular way to move around in your virtual world. This is a concept where the user points at a location in the virtual world, and is transported to that location automatically on the press of a button.
There are 2 main types of teleportation namely, instant teleportation and dash teleportation that you will explore in this lecture.
Instant teleportation, is where, when a valid teleportable area is found, the user will instantly be repositioned at this point, usually with a camera fade, to reduce motion sickness.
Dash teleportation, is where, when a valid teleportable area is found, the user is gradually moved over time in a linear motion, until the specific teleportable point is reached.
You will also setup a curved pointer to facilitate teleportation.
VIDEO LECTURE: SETTING up the Teleporter
In the last lecture, you setup your curved pointer to facilitate teleportation. In this lecture, you will set up the Teleporter object, which will enable you teleport around within your Virtual world.
While the curved pointer is responsible for recognizing a valid teleport and initiating it, the Teleporter is responsible for moving the Play area to its new location, which essentially moves your Virtual Player to this new location.
VIDEO LECTURE: SETTING up Teleport Targets to traverse large Distances
Some VR experiences won’t allow you to teleport everywhere. Instead, they rely on fixed hot spots, or teleport targets, to limit the players movement. This is ideal for creating immersive experiences, and puzzle games like Mist and Riven.
You can create such behavior using teleport targets. These Teleport targets are VRTK prefabs, that earmark certain specific points or areas, that you can teleport too.
Also, should your VR experience require you to traverse large terrains, teleport targets positioned strategically, will help create a great VR experience.
VRTK allows you to achieve this Targeted Teleportation, by providing you with 2 prefabs, namely, a Teleport Targets Point, and a Teleport Targets area.
In this lecture, you will setup both types of Teleport targets.
VIDEO LECTURE: SLIDE Movement and Rotation
Another neat movement type provided by VRTK, is the ability to move around your scene, by simply gliding along the floor surface, by either moving forward, backward, or strafing left and right.
In this lecture you will setup free-flowing movement and rotation, where you can slide yourself around by pushing your left thumb stick, forward and backward as well as rotate around, by pushing your left thumb stick, left or right.
VIDEO LECTURE: ARM Swinging Locomotion
Teleporting, and sliding around your world, are great, but in some cases, letting the player swing their arms, to move, can increase the level of immersion.
This enables them to decide, how fast they want to move, and allows for small accurate movements.
If you were creating an endless runner, game in VR, arm swinging would be a truly immersive and natural way, to move yourself in the world.
Arm swinging is also referred to as Move in Place locomotion and in this lecture you will setup this form of locomotion.
VIDEO LECTURE: SETTING up a Pseudo Body
In the last 2 lectures on Sliding and arm swinging movement, you would have noticed that you could easily pass thru the solid containers, barrels, Ladder and Table that are available in the scene.
In most cases you would not want such a behavior.
Ideally you would want yourself to be stopped the moment you collided with a solid object.
To ensure that you do not pass thru solid objects, you need to set up a Pseudo body.
This pseudo body will allow you to provide a virtual presence, to your real-world body position, within a spatial scene.

CHAPTER EIGHT
VRTK's 3D Spatial UI and Unity's 2D UI controls
THE USER INTERFACE , is an important part of any game.
It informs the user about their health, score, as well as helps to control the games work flow, via the use of menus and pause screens.
In a non-VR game, the UI used is referred to as a Heads-Up Display, or HUD, which is placed over the top of the main screen and shows information to the player.
This type of UI is known as a non-diegetic UI, meaning it doesn’t exist physically in the game world.
However, it directly relates to what the player sees or interacts with, inside the game.
Unfortunately, this non-diegetic UI approach doesn’t work well in VR, as the player is unable to focus on something so close to their eyes.
It is very distracting and uncomfortable, as it moves with the players head, as if it has been glued on.
A better and more intuitive way, is to have the UI positioned within the game environment.
This can be achieved by using Unity’s UI, based on a world space UI canvas. This approach is referred to as Spatial UI.
With VRTK version 4 however, you now have the ability to create 3 D user interfaces, by using spatial buttons, that can be interacted with via interactors, or pointers.
In this section, you will look at interacting with 3D spatial buttons, that exist as objects within your game world, using a spatial pointer as well as directly with your hands.
VIDEO LECTURE: SETTING up a Straight Pointer
In this lecture, you will setup a straight pointer that will be exclusively used for interacting with your Spatial buttons only.
The straight pointer you set up here is different from the one setup for the distance grab.
Also, a different button on your controller will be used to activate this straight spatial pointer.
As you may have noted, while setting up your distance grab, a straight pointer, casts a straight line, from the palm of your hand, to either its maximum specified length, or until the pointer beam, collided with a valid game object.
When a straight pointer, collides with a valid game object, a selection action can occur, emitting an event, containing data about the game object the straight pointer, collided with.
You will use this to create a straight pointer that can interact with your spatial buttons.
VIDEO LECTURE: SETTING up a Spatial, Clickable Button

Angular and Linear Drives
IN THIS SECTION , you will learn about the Angular and Linear drive prefabs, provided by VRTK, as well as see several examples of their use.
You will start off using an Angular drive, wherein you will create a steering wheel, a door, and a lever.
You will then proceed to use a Linear drive, which you have already used when you created your slider object. You will however use this linear drive prefab again, to create a drawer as well as a push button.
Both angular and linear drives, are referred to as controllable mechanisms, that provide you with both, physics and non-physics based, angular and linear interactable controls, that can be used with a wide variety of objects.
You will use the non-physics-based controls, wherever possible, as the physics-based controls rely heavily on joints and if you intend building multi player games, its best to keep joints, to their bare minimum.

Mini Game
IN THIS FINAL section of the course, you will create a small mini game.
The game you will create is a simplistic, Katamari style clone game, where the objective of the game is to roll a ball about the garden path, collecting the strewn objects, until the time runs out.
